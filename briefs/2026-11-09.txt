Nvidia's board investigates Jensen Huang over an affair with a suspected Chinese spy

The CEO of the company that makes the chips the entire AI industry runs on apologized to staff and is under an independent-director probe over possible espionage exposure, while Google DeepMind used AI agents to break long-standing assumptions about legacy 1024-bit RSA encryption and OpenAI published a cross-industry framework for rating how dangerous a jailbreak really is.

01 · Nvidia's board investigates Jensen Huang over an affair with a suspected Chinese spy
Per Bloomberg, Nvidia's board announced Sunday it is launching an investigation into whether CEO Jensen Huang was targeted by a foreign espionage plot, after Huang admitted to staff that he had been unfaithful to his wife. US intelligence officials, per reporting cited by multiple outlets including NBC News, determined that the woman involved — a businesswoman whose company was buying Nvidia's advanced chips — was working for Chinese intelligence. Nvidia's statement said it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised, but the board is assembling a committee of independent directors working with outside counsel to review the case. Separately, WIRED reported that Huang had been working with Heller, a crisis-communications firm known for handling scandals, since October 2025 — roughly a year before the affair became public.
Signal: this isn't a product story but it's the single biggest thing that happened to the AI industry's most important hardware company this week — Nvidia's board is now assembling independent directors and outside counsel specifically to assess whether national-security-sensitive information or chip-export compliance was exposed, not just a personal-conduct issue.

02 · Google DeepMind uses AI agents to break assumptions behind legacy RSA encryption
Per Google DeepMind's own research post (via VP Science Pushmeet Kohli) and Interesting Engineering's reporting, DeepMind used AI agents, guided by human researchers, to find two weaknesses in legacy 1024-bit RSA cryptosystems: a new algorithm that makes forging a signature theoretically about 1,600x cheaper once an attacker controls roughly half of a signed data block, and a batch-factorization technique that cuts the cost of cracking a batch of keys by about 30x per key. DeepMind is explicit that modern 2048-bit systems remain safe from these specific exploits, and frames the result as evidence that AI-enabled math now requires continuous, not occasional, stress-testing of cryptographic standards.
Signal: a genuine capability result with security implications, carefully bounded by DeepMind itself — modern 2048-bit RSA is unaffected, but the finding shows AI agents can now meaningfully accelerate cryptanalysis, which changes how fast institutions need to react when a cryptographic assumption starts to crack.
Source: https://interestingengineering.com/ai-robotics/google-deepmind-agents-expose-older-encryption-flaws

03 · OpenAI and industry peers publish a shared framework for rating cyber jailbreaks
Per the Frontier Model Forum's own announcement, Version 1.0 of its Jailbreak Rating Framework for Frontier Cyber Threats scores a successful jailbreak across four categories — capability gain, capability scope, ease of deployment and ease of discovery — to make severity assessments comparable across different labs' findings. OpenAI's Sasha Baker, head of National Security Policy, said the framework is "an important step toward a common methodology" as models grow more capable. The draft is open for public comment until December 21.
Signal: notable for being a joint industry standard rather than a single-lab announcement — the Frontier Model Forum (whose members include OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind and Microsoft) is trying to give labs and governments a common vocabulary for "how bad is this jailbreak," which has been missing even as jailbreak disclosures have become routine.
Source: https://www.frontiermodelforum.org/updates/introducing-the-fmf-jailbreak-rating-framework-for-frontier-cyber-threats

04 · OpenAI benchmarks AI agents on raw wet-lab science data
Per OpenAI's own account and reporting from Unite.AI and R&D World, OpenAI built BioBasicsBench with enzyme-design startup Ligo Biosciences: 100 tasks drawn from Ligo's real experiments, each reviewed by an outside PhD-level wet-lab scientist before being used to grade models. Across 37 model and reasoning-effort settings, GPT-6 Astra scored the highest mean reward at 66.7% on its highest reasoning setting, with over-interpretation and basic counting still tripping models up.
Signal: a deliberately unglamorous benchmark — rather than testing on curated figures from published papers, BioBasicsBench grades agents on messy real lab data (gel photos, microscopy, sequencing reads) and explicitly rewards saying "the evidence doesn't support a conclusion" over a confident wrong answer.

05 · Anthropic details "dreaming," how Claude's agents organize their own memory
Per Anthropic's own engineering blog post, "dreaming" is a background process that gives Claude time to look back over what it learned in a session, correct errors in its notes, and reorganize what it keeps so future sessions can find relevant memory more easily. Anthropic says the technique has already shown value in practice, including when Claude played Pokémon using a mounted-directory memory setup, and frames it as one of several components (alongside salience judgment and note-taking) that make up how its agents learn from experience.
Signal: a concrete engineering mechanism rather than a research claim — "dreaming" lets Claude re-check its own memory notes against the original conversation transcripts between sessions, catching and correcting its own mistakes rather than just accumulating them.
Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/components-of-agent-memory
