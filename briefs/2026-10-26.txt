Two of Claude Code's most visible engineers leave Anthropic in the same week, and Elvis Presley's estate sues Anthropic over 274 songs

A rough few days for Anthropic on two fronts — Thariq Shihipar and Anthony Morris, both key builders of Claude Code, left within a day of each other, while Authentic Brands Group sued the company for roughly $41M over training Claude on 274 unlicensed Elvis Presley songs.

01 · Two key Claude Code engineers, Thariq Shihipar and Anthony Morris, leave Anthropic within a day of each other
Per Thariq Shihipar's own X post (confirmed by multiple outlets), he left Anthropic on October 24 after more than a year on Claude Code, calling it "the most fun yet stressful job" and saying he's taking a break before starting something new; he did not name a next role. Anthony Morris, who built the Claude Code desktop app, separately said October 23 was his last day. Both posts were warm toward Anthropic and its mission, and colleagues like Lydia Hallie publicly thanked them — this reads as an amicable, not acrimonious, pair of exits.
Signal: notable less for either individual exit and more for the overlap — two of the most publicly visible builders of Claude Code (one on the core engineering side, one who built the desktop app specifically) leaving in the same week, right as Anthropic is in IPO-prep mode, invites more scrutiny than either departure would alone. Anthropic has not commented on either.

02 · Elvis Presley's estate sues Anthropic for roughly $41M, alleging Claude was trained on 274 unlicensed songs
Per the lawsuit (filed by Authentic Brands Group and reported by the LA Times, NBC News and Rolling Stone), Anthropic is accused of training Claude on 274 Presley songs — including "All Shook Up," "Can't Help Falling in Love," "Don't Be Cruel" and "Return to Sender" — without a license or payment. Plaintiffs seek statutory damages up to $150,000 per song (roughly $41M total), a permanent injunction, and destruction of unauthorized copies; they also allege Anthropic continued the practice after being notified it was improperly accessing the material. Anthropic has previously argued that training on copyrighted material can qualify as fair use, but hasn't yet responded to this suit specifically.
Signal: a new front in AI copyright litigation — Anthropic already paid a record $1.5B to settle a books-training lawsuit with authors, and this is one of the first cases where a music estate (rather than a label or the RIAA) has sued an AI company directly, naming specific hit songs rather than making a generic claim.
