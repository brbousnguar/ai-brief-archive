Anthropic commits 5% of its shares to a new public benefit fund ahead of a possible IPO, and Linus Torvalds shuts down calls to ban AI in the Linux kernel

Anthropic is giving up a meaningful equity stake to a new public-benefit entity just as it works to build investor confidence toward a listing, while Linus Torvalds flatly rejected banning AI-assisted code from the Linux kernel — two different angles on AI's growing institutional weight.

01 · Anthropic commits 5% of its shares to a new Anthropic Public Benefit Fund
Per Anthropic's own announcement (confirmed by Reuters and the Wall Street Journal), the company will commit 5% of its shares — pending stockholder approval — to a new wholly owned entity, the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, with an additional 2% added at the board's discretion for every 50% increase in the company's value above a yet-undisclosed baseline. The fund can make large donations to nonprofits or direct distributions to specific populations; Anthropic cited AI's labor-market impact as one target, building on the $200M it already committed to studying that issue. Combined with co-founders' earlier 80% equity-donation pledge and an employee donation-match program, Anthropic says roughly 20% of its shares are now earmarked for public-benefit purposes.
Signal: this lands right as Anthropic is reportedly building investor confidence ahead of a potential public listing, making the timing as notable as the commitment itself — giving up equity value right before trying to court public markets is an unusual signal, whichever way investors read it (dilution cost vs. credibility signal).
Source: https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/anthropic-to-commit-5-of-its-shares-to-new-public-benefit-fund-3332ef75

02 · Linus Torvalds rejects calls to ban AI-assisted code from the Linux kernel, and the "Assisted-by" disclosure tag gets dropped
Per reporting on the kernel mailing list thread (lore.kernel.org), kernel developer Sasha Levin proposed dropping the "Assisted-by" AI-attribution requirement after it proved hard to enforce; when developer Laurent Pinchart argued clearer AI-use expectations were still needed, Torvalds rejected the idea of restricting tools at all, comparing an AI ban to banning compilers: "if you can't show that you really understand what the machine is actually doing... I don't want to see your code contribution." Maintainers, including Greg Kroah-Hartman (who'd previously backed the tag), now support removing it.
Signal: a real-world test case for how open-source governance handles AI, not an AI-industry announcement — Torvalds' argument (competence matters, not tool choice) settled a dispute that started when the kernel's "Assisted-by" AI-disclosure tag, introduced only this year, was judged largely unenforced and got proposed for removal.
